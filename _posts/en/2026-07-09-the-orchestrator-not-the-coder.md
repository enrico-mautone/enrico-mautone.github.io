*Originally published in Italian on July 9, 2026, a few weeks before I started as CTO.*

In September I'm making a career jump: from service and delivery management in enterprise consulting to CTO of a brand-new AI startup. On paper it sounds like the usual story — the manager who finally goes back to being technical. It isn't. And explaining why is the reason this blog exists.

## The moment I changed my mind about AI

For the past few months I've been studying agentic AI from scratch, mostly by following creators who use it every day to build things, not just to talk about it. One idea turned my way of seeing things upside down, borrowed from Austin Marchese: **rent it, don't own it.**

AI is no longer just an assistant that helps you write code faster or automate a repetitive task on your computer. The paradigm is shifting: AI agents are becoming the ones who actually do the work. We are becoming the ones who decide *what* needs to be done, and *whether* it was done well.

## Orchestrator, not executor

This reframing changes how I think about the CTO role I'm about to take on. I no longer see it as "the most senior engineer in the room". I see it like this:

> My first employee is an AI agent.

I don't write the code — even though I review every line. My job is to point that agent in the direction the company needs, check its work, correct the course, and repeat. Execution is delegated; judgment is not.

## What changes — for developers, architects and CTOs

If agents are becoming the executors, every technical role built around "the person who writes the code" needs rethinking:

- **Developers** move from writing implementations to directing and verifying them — the skill that matters is recognizing what "correct" looks like, not typing speed.
- **Software architects** matter *more*, not less: when execution is delegated, the quality of the design you feed it becomes the ceiling of what gets built.
- **CTOs** end up closer to someone managing a non-human workforce than to a senior engineer — technical leadership redefined around direction and verification, not direct output.

Let me be honest: this isn't a settled thesis I'm handing down. It's a live hypothesis I'm testing on a real project, and I expect to get parts of it wrong.

## Why I'm writing this

This blog won't be a collection of things I've already figured out. It's the log of a transition in progress — from today until September, when the new role officially starts, I'll write about what I'm learning and what I'm getting wrong, as it happens.

I'm doing it in public because the place I'm writing from — someone with a traditional software background moving toward directing AI agents instead of writing code by hand — feels like a place many other people are about to find themselves in, whether they've noticed or not. If my notes save someone else a few of my mistakes, this blog will have done its job.
